Law of Blood Compensation

The prerequisite for robbery is that there must be something to steal; the prerequisite for kidnapping and demanding ransom is that the hostage must have the financial capacity to pay the ransom. As the Law of Blood Payment (or Law of Life Compensation) states, the value of the hostage’s life is determined by the willingness and ability of the parties to pay the ransom. Under conditions of equal risk and cost, the richer the hostage, and the wealthier the target of the robbery, the higher the profit from the kidnapping and robbery. Conversely, the poorer the target of the robbery and kidnapping, the lower the profit from the robbery. If the profit is so low that it is not worth the effort, the bandits cannot proceed.

The warlord zones of the late Qing Dynasty frequently changed hands, implementing a policy of “killing the chicken to take the eggs” (killing the goose to get the gander/harvesting immediately), but the negative consequences might not necessarily be borne by oneself, and strength can still be bolstered. Implementing a policy of “raising the chicken to lay eggs” (nurturing for future gain), one might not necessarily be able to eat the eggs in the future, and the chickens might be stolen and eaten by others; the current malnutrition could also rapidly lead to collapse. Within this framework, “draining the marsh to catch the fish” (exhausting resources for immediate gain) — the standard behavior of bandits/marauders — is the most advantageous choice.

In the mid-to-late Ming Dynasty, due to the development of the socio-economy and population growth, farmers’ demand for land increased. However, the uneven distribution of land and the heavy burden of taxes made it impossible for many farmers to maintain a livelihood. In the mid-to-late Ming Dynasty, due to the corruption of officials and lax supervision, the management of the Yellow Registers and travel permits became chaotic and riddled with loopholes. What are the defining changes as a dynasty moves from its middle phase to its final years?

As a dynasty moves from its middle period towards its late years, it is often accompanied by a series of characteristic changes. Here are some common changes: Political Instability and Power Struggles: As time passes, the dynasty’s governance often experiences instability and power struggles. Political corruption, unfair official appointments, and the phenomenon of vying for power may intensify, leading to internal political instability and chaos. Territorial Retreat and War Challenges: In its late years, the dynasty may face territorial shrinkage, losing control over certain regions. Ming Dynasty Core Values

As early as the Ming Dynasty, village heads and street entrances across various regions were erected with “Six Precepts Lying Steles” (or “Six Teachings Reclining Steles”). The content consists of the “Twenty-Four Characters of Moral Norms” stipulated by the Ming Taizu, Zhu Yuanzhang, for the common people: “Filial piety to parents, respect for elders, harmony in the village, educating children, ensuring everyone’s livelihood, and avoiding wrongful deeds.”

To seize when favorable, and to defend when unfavorable.

Before ascending the throne, Zhu’s forces were always the “Army of Benevolence and Righteousness” (Renyi Zhi Shi), and Zhu himself was always a model of practicing the “King’s Way” (Wang Dao). Consequently, the people willingly submitted their hearts and allegiance. Who would have thought that after conquering the realm and incorporating the entire populace into his cage, he would transform from being generous and benevolent to being harsh and cruel, raising the whip and the butcher’s knife to slaughter the world, massacring meritorious officials, and becoming a demonic king that made everyone tremble with fear? In fact, it was not Zhu Yuanzhang’s nature that changed, but the circumstances. Before ascending the throne, he sought support from various social classes; he needed to gain their backing. After ascending the throne, the common people had become the cattle and sheep under his pastoral care, and naturally, he was happy to command them forward with the butcher’s knife and the whip.
