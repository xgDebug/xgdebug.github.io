Rebalancing between aggressive and defensive stocks

1. Basic Concepts of Rebalancing Asset Allocation: Investors set the proportion of different asset classes (e.g., stocks, bonds, cash) based on their risk tolerance and return goals. Market Fluctuation: Market fluctuations can cause asset proportions to deviate from the target (e.g., a rise in stocks might make their share in the portfolio too high). Rebalancing: Adjusting the portfolio back to the target proportion by buying and selling assets. 2. Methods of Rebalancing Time-based Rebalancing: Adjusting the portfolio at fixed time intervals (e.g., quarterly or annually). Ratio-based Rebalancing: Adjusting when a specific asset class deviates from the target proportion by more than a certain threshold (e.g., 5%). 3. Advantages of Rebalancing Risk Control: Prevents a single asset from having too high a proportion, thereby reducing risk. Discipline: Avoids emotional trading, maintaining the stability of the investment strategy. Return Optimization: May enhance long-term returns by buying low and selling high. 4. Disadvantages of Rebalancing Transaction Costs: Frequent adjustments may increase trading fees. Tax Implications: In taxable accounts, rebalancing may generate capital gains tax. Market Trends: In a unidirectional market (trend), rebalancing may reduce returns. 5. Implementation of Rebalancing Determine Target Proportion: Setting asset allocation based on risk preference. Monitor Portfolio: Regularly checking asset proportions. Execute Adjustment: Restoring the target proportion by buying and selling assets. Summary Rebalancing is a tool for “exchanging short-term transaction costs for long-term risk control.” It is suitable for volatile or sector-rotating markets, but may incur opportunity costs in unidirectional trend markets. When executing, it is recommended to combine threshold rules (e.g., rebalancing after a 10% deviation) rather than mechanically operating based on time. Furthermore, it is recommended to operate a maximum of twice a year.

Taking an uncertain world as the investment premise

We should spend time trying to find value in knowable factors such as industries, companies, and securities, rather than making decisions based on our predictions about the relatively unknowable overall economic world and market trends. Because we cannot know the exact shape of the future, we must acquire value through methods such as firm conviction, views derived from future analysis, and buying in at a lower price when opportunities arise. We must adopt defensive investing because many outcomes may be unfavorable to us. More importantly, we must confirm the ability to survive in the market under negative outcomes, rather than guaranteeing maximum returns under favorable outcomes. To increase the chances of success, when extreme situations occur, we must emphasize that the actions taken should be contrary to the crowd: being aggressive when the market is sluggish, and being cautious when the market is euphoric. Because the results are inherently highly uncertain, unless they have been verified through many experiments, we must view the decisions and the resulting outcomes with a skeptical perspective—whether they are good outcomes or bad outcomes.
