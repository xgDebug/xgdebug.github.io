Grand History of Humanity

The article explores how humans have lived between objective reality and imagined reality since the Cognitive Revolution, and discusses how the order constructed by imagination profoundly influences human society, desires, and behavior.
December 28, 2024 · 11 min · xgDebug | Translations:
  • 中文

Since the Cognitive Revolution, humanity has lived in a dual reality. On one hand, we have the objective reality that truly exists, such as rivers, trees, and lions; on the other hand, we have the imagined reality, such as God, the nation, and the corporation.

One of the few unshakable truths throughout history is that initial luxuries often eventually become necessities, bringing with them new obligations. Once we become accustomed to a certain luxury, we begin to believe it is natural or self-evident. This leads to a dependency. Eventually, life cannot proceed without that luxury.

Whether it is Christianity, democracy, or capitalism, they are all orders built by imagination. But how do we make people believe in these orders?

First, the external assertion must absolutely insist that they are certain and absolutely not fictional. We must always emphasize that this order, which maintains social stability, is an objective fact created by a great God or by natural law.

Second, the same principles must be thoroughly implemented in education. From the moment a person is born, they must be constantly reminded that this order, built by imagination, must be integrated into everything—whether it is fairy tales, plays, paintings, songs, rituals, political propaganda, architecture, recipes, or fashion.

The existence of “subjective” things relies on the consciousness and belief of a single individual. If this person changes their belief, this subjective thing ceases to exist, or changes along with it. For example, many children imagine they have a friend only they can see and hear. This imagined friend exists only in the child’s subjective consciousness; when the child grows up and stops believing, this friend vanishes from the world.

The existence of “intersubjectivity” relies on the network of connections between many individual subjective consciousnesses. Even if one person changes their mind, or even passes away, the impact on this phenomenon is not great. But if the majority of people in this network die, or change their minds, this intersubjectivity will disappear, or change.

Even if I personally resolve to stop believing in the dollar, human rights, and America, it cannot cause any change. Precisely because these orders built by imagination possess intersubjectivity, if we want to change them, we must simultaneously change the thoughts of billions of people—this is by no means easy. To achieve such large-scale change, complex organizations must assist behind the scenes, perhaps political parties, perhaps ideological movements, or perhaps a certain religious sect.
