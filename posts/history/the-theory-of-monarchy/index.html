The Theory of Monarchy

Explore the various aspects of monarchical rule, including military, morality, generosity, cruelty, and how to avoid being hated and despised by the people.

Chapter Nine: The Citizen Monarchy#

Those who gain monarchical power through the support of the populace find it more difficult to maintain their existing status than those who acquire it with the aid of the nobility. This is because the monarch is surrounded by many individuals who believe they are on equal footing with him, which prevents him from ruling or managing them according to his own wishes.

Chapter Fourteen: The Monarch’s Duties in Military Affairs#

Those who are armed and those who are unarmed cannot be compared. Do not expect the armed to willingly obey the unarmed, nor should you expect the unarmed, when surrounded by armed servants, to feel completely at ease. One side harbors suspicion while the other harbors contempt; these two parties cannot cooperate seamlessly. Therefore, a ruler, aside from the other misfortunes already mentioned, if he does not understand military affairs, he cannot win the respect of his soldiers, nor can he rely on their loyalty.

Chapter Fifteen: Reasons Why People, Especially Monarchs, Are Praised or Condemned#

To study how things should be done while ignoring how things actually are done—such a person cannot save himself; he will only bring about his own ruin.

Those who boast of benevolence and righteousness in all matters, once placed among the common rabble, will inevitably bring trouble upon themselves. Thus, if a monarch wishes to survive, he must learn not to be a pushover (or “goody-two-shoes”). He must also look at the actual situation to decide whether to apply this realization.

He must possess sufficient wisdom to know how to avoid the wicked deeds and bad reputations that might lead to the loss of his regime. If possible, he should be vigilant to avoid acquiring a bad reputation that could threaten his power. If that is not possible, it is fine to indulge a little.

Even if it is a bad thing, if he does not do it, it will be difficult to maintain his regime, so he must resolutely do it, without worrying that the bad reputation will invite condemnation from others.

Some things appear to be virtues, but when put into action, they bring about destruction; and some things appear to be evils, but when put into action, they can guarantee the monarch’s safety and the welfare of the people.

Chapter Sixteen: Generosity and Stinginess#

Everyone believes that one should perform good deeds without seeking recognition. If a monarch truly acts this way, no one will see his generosity. As a result, he will inevitably be criticized for being stingy. Thus, to maintain a reputation for generosity, the monarch must increase the burden on the people, using every possible means to levy heavy taxes and miscellaneous fees, which in turn breeds popular resentment. When his coffers become tight, even people will stop respecting him.

Because his generosity offends many people, it only satisfies an extremely small number. He begins to realize the signs of shifting public sentiment; the slightest breeze can threaten his regime, so he decides to change his style. However, as soon as his style changes, the bad reputation of stinginess immediately falls upon him.

A wise monarch should not worry about the bad reputation of stinginess. After years, when people see that the national treasury is abundant, that there are resources to defend against external threats, and that active construction can proceed without heavy levies, the people who are not oppressed by him will consider him generous—and these people are the majority.

Those who achieve great feats are often the monarchs who are perceived as stingy; the monarchs who are perceived as generous are often those who achieve nothing.

It is said that to avoid exploiting the people’s wealth, to ensure his own safety, and to prevent the treasury from being empty and him being looked down upon, a monarch should not mind being called a miser, because stinginess is one of the vices that can help him govern.

You may already be a monarch, or you may be about to become one. If it is the former case, generosity is harmful to you; if it is the latter case, it is absolutely necessary for others to perceive you as generous. Caesar is one of those who aspire to be a Roman monarch, so he must display generosity; but once he achieves his goal, if he is lucky enough to survive and does not restrain his spending, he will destroy the power he gained with his own hands.

The funds for a monarch’s expenditures may come from himself and his subjects, or they may come from others. If it is the former case, he should economize; if it is the latter case, he should display generosity at all times and in all places. When the monarch is leading troops in the field, relying on plunder, spoils, and ransoms to maintain expenses, the wealth he handles belongs to others, so he must fully demonstrate generosity, otherwise his soldiers will not follow him.

There is nothing in the world that depletes oneself more easily than generosity.

Chapter Seventeen: Cruelty and Benevolence: Which is More Advantageous—Being Loved or Being Feared?#

As long as cruelty can maintain the unity and loyalty of the subjects, the monarch should not mind the bad reputation of cruelty. Compared to excessive benevolence leading to long-term disorder, which then triggers murder and robbery, causing harm to the entire society, a handful of brutal incidents are clearly more benevolent, because the monarch’s order to execute only harms the individual. Ultimately, is being loved preferable to being feared, or vice versa? The answer is that both are equally important and should not be neglected. However, since one cannot have both the fish and the bear’s paw, and given that both harms are equally powerful, naturally, being feared offers much greater security than being loved.

This is because, generally speaking, people are ungrateful, fickle, prone to making grand promises, inherently hypocritical, inclined to avoid danger and seek comfort, and insatiable. If you give them benefits, they will incline toward you. As I mentioned earlier, when hardship is still distant, they are willing to go through fire and water for you—they can dedicate their wealth, their lives, even their sons to you; but when the critical moment arrives, they will turn and leave, without even looking back.

The people’s love for the monarch stems from the will of the people; but the key to the people’s fear of the monarch lies in the monarch’s own will. He must be able to be the master of himself. Therefore, a wise monarch should follow only his own will, and not rely on the will of others. As long as he strives to avoid popular resentment, just as mentioned earlier, that will suffice.

Chapter Eighteen: The Way of the Monarch to Keep Promises#

Once the reason for his own benefit or the original promise disappears, a wise monarch should neither keep nor should he keep the promise.

Humans are inherently wicked; they will not keep their promises to you. By the same logic, you have no need to keep your promises to them.

There will never be a lack of a legitimate reason to gloss over his act of breaking a promise. Just look at how many treaties have become worthless paper, and how many promises have become empty words—it is all due to the monarch being inconsistent between his words and his heart.

One must know how to beautifully conceal his beastly nature and become a great liar and hypocrite. People are very naive; they only consider immediate needs, which allows the deceiver to always find someone willing to be fooled.

A monarch does not necessarily have to possess all those qualities considered good earlier, but it is indeed necessary for people to feel that he possesses them. I dare to say even: possessing those qualities and practicing them at all times and in all places will certainly harm oneself; conversely, making people feel that he possesses all those qualities is very useful—for example, making people feel that he is compassionate, trustworthy, humane, upright, and possesses devout faith, and making them believe that he practices them. But the monarch must be prepared, and when necessary, he must know how to change his course.

He must always conform to the prevailing winds of fortune and the changes in the situation. Furthermore, as I mentioned earlier, if possible, he should not neglect virtue, but when necessary, he should know how to commit wrongdoing.

He must always present himself in such a way that people feel or see that he is the embodiment of compassion, trustworthy, upright, humane, and devoutly faithful. The last point is especially necessary because humans usually make judgments based on what they see, not what they touch; because everyone can see, but only a few can have direct contact. Everyone sees how you present yourself, but few can perceive your true nature, and those few dare not go against the majority.

Chapter Nineteen: How to Avoid Being Despised and Resented#

A monarch cannot please both sides; accommodating one side will inevitably offend the other. Therefore, first and foremost, he should strive to avoid causing widespread popular resentment; if this cannot be done, as a secondary measure, he should strive to avoid causing the resentment of the most powerful group of people.

Chapter Twenty: Pros and Cons of Measures Commonly Used by Monarchs, Such as Fortifications#

One must carefully consider the motives of those people. If their motives are not genuine affection, but merely dissatisfaction with the old regime, then the new monarch’s effort to maintain a friendly relationship with them will inevitably be arduous and difficult, because it is impossible to satisfy them.

Those who were initially hostile toward the old regime because they were satisfied with it are easier to win over; conversely, those who helped him occupy the area because they were dissatisfied with the old regime are difficult to deal with.

Chapter Twenty-Two: The Monarch’s Key Ministers#

When we judge the intelligence of a master, the initial impression comes from observing the people around him. If the people around him are capable and loyal, we can say that the person is wise with about 80-90% certainty. This is because he has the ability to discern people, allowing talented individuals to rise to prominence, and he deeply understands how to utilize them, ensuring their unwavering loyalty. However, if the people around him are mediocre or opportunistic, our negative impression of him will not be wrong, because his first major mistake is poor personnel selection.

Speaking of human talent, there are no more than three types: one is inherently capable of understanding; another is capable of discerning the understanding of others; and the third is inherently incapable of understanding, yet unable to benefit from the understanding of others.

How does a monarch discern the good from the bad in his ministers? There is a reliable method here: those who think only of themselves and rarely think of the monarch, whose actions are always for their own benefit, will not become good ministers. You can never trust them, because they hold the power entrusted to them, and they should not only consider themselves, but should always consider the monarch, thinking of the monarch’s interests above all else.

Chapter Twenty-Three: How to Avoid Flatterers#

People are naturally prone to feeling overly pleased with themselves, thus indulging in self-admiration, and it is difficult to resist this plague. Attempts to resist it carry the risk of being looked down upon. The reason for this is that there is only one way to guard against flattery: to let people know that telling you the truth will not offend you. However, once everyone tells you the truth, you will lose universal respect.

A wise monarch should adopt a third method: selecting wise people as his ministers, allowing only those ministers the freedom to tell him the truth, and only allowing them to answer his questions, and nothing else. However, the monarch should inquire about their opinions in minute detail, listen carefully, and then make his decision after deep consideration.
