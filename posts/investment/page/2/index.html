Taking an uncertain world as the investment premise

We should spend time trying to find value in knowable factors such as industries, companies, and securities, rather than making decisions based on our predictions about the relatively unknowable overall economic world and market trends. Because we cannot know the exact shape of the future, we must acquire value through methods such as firm conviction, views derived from future analysis, and buying in at a lower price when opportunities arise. We must adopt defensive investing because many outcomes may be unfavorable to us. More importantly, we must confirm the ability to survive in the market under negative outcomes, rather than guaranteeing maximum returns under favorable outcomes. To increase the chances of success, when extreme situations occur, we must emphasize that the actions taken should be contrary to the crowd: being aggressive when the market is sluggish, and being cautious when the market is euphoric. Because the results are inherently highly uncertain, unless they have been verified through many experiments, we must view the decisions and the resulting outcomes with a skeptical perspective—whether they are good outcomes or bad outcomes.

Second-Level Thinking

Investment Thinking Must Be Different The exceptional insights, intuition, value concepts, and understanding of market psychology required to maintain returns above average. To achieve this, you need second-level thinking. Doing the right thing might be a necessary condition for successful investing, but it is not a sufficient condition. You must do more things right than others, which means your way of thinking must be different. What is Second-Level Thinking? First-level thinking says: “This is a good company, so buy this stock!” Second-level thinking says: “This is a good company, but everyone thinks this company is good, so it isn’t a good company. This stock is overvalued, the market price is too high, so sell!” First-level thinking says: “From a forward-looking perspective, economic growth is sluggish and inflation is rising, so sell the holdings!” Second-level thinking says: “The economic outlook is terrible, but everyone is selling stocks out of panic, so you should buy!” First-level thinking says: “I believe this company’s earnings will decline, so sell!” Second-level thinking says: “I believe the decline in this company’s earnings will be less than expected; an unexpected surprise will boost the stock price, so buy!” People Who Practice Second-Level Thinking Consider Many Things What range of outcomes might occur in the future? Which outcome do I believe will occur? How high is the probability that my view is correct? What is the market consensus? How large is the difference between my expectation and the market consensus? How closely does the market price of this asset align with the price believed by the market consensus? And what about my own price? Is the consensus sentiment reflected in the price overly optimistic or pessimistic? If the market consensus proves to be correct, what effect will that have on the asset price? If my expectation is the correct one, what effect will that have? There is a clear and significant difference in the workload between first-level thinking and second-level thinking. The number of people capable of second-level thinking is obviously much smaller than those capable of first-level thinking. First-level thinkers look for simple formulas and easy answers. Second-level thinkers know that successful investing is contrary to simplicity. This doesn’t mean you won’t encounter many people who try to make investing sound simple, I call them “mercenaries.” Securities companies want you to believe that everyone is capable of investing, and that every transaction only costs ten dollars. Fund companies don’t want you to believe that you are capable of investing, but they want you to believe that they are capable of investing, so you will put your money into actively managed funds and pay higher management fees.
