More than huge returns, the safety of wealth is more important.#

  • Extreme success largely depends on luck.
  • Investing and financial management isn’t entirely luck. It might be a bit like playing cards—there is a considerable element of luck, but there is also a certain level of skill.

Chapter 1: Nobody Truly Loses Their Mind Over Money#

In the United States, the lowest-income households spend an average of $412 per year on lottery tickets, which is four times the amount spent by high-income households. 40% of Americans cannot pull out even $400 when they desperately need money. This means that those who spend $400 on lottery tickets are basically the people who cannot produce $400 when they need it most. They are betting the $400 that could provide them with security on a chance of winning that is only one in a million.

Few people make financial decisions simply based on electronic reports. Decisions are often made while people are dining or in meetings. In these settings, personal experiences, unique worldviews, subjective self, pride, marketing tactics, and extraordinary incentives all work together to form the context that prompts you to make a decision.

The 401K plan—America’s main retirement system—only appeared in 1978; the Roth IRA was born in 1998. If we consider the latter a person, then he has just reached the age of legal drinking.

Chapter 4: The Mystery of Compound Interest#

Warren Buffett’s net worth is $84.5 billion, of which $84.2 billion was earned after the age of 50, and $81.5 billion was earned after the age of 65—he earned this much starting from the age when he could receive a pension.

If he had started investing around the age of 30 and retired at 60, few people would know about it.

Buffett treated investing as a career from the age of 10. By the age of 30, he had a net worth of $1 million. Considering inflation, this amount is equivalent to $9.3 million today. ￼

If he were just an ordinary person, exploring the world and searching for passion like most people at the age of 10 or 20, how much wealth would he have by the age of 30? Perhaps $25,000?

What if this wealth continued to grow at an excellent annual rate (22%), but he ended his investment career at 60, starting to spend his time playing golf and spoiling his grandchildren?

How much would his wealth be today?

Not $84.5 billion.

But only $11.9 million.

This figure is 99.9% less than his current net worth.

Chapter 5: Wealth vs. Wealth Preservation#

The fact that “the long-term result is positive, but the short-term process may be terrible” seems counterintuitive at first glance, but many things in life are indeed like this. When everyone reaches the age of 20, the number of synapses in their brain shrinks to half of what it was at age 2, because inefficient and redundant neural connections are cleared, but clearly, a 20-year-old is much smarter than a 2-year-old. Destruction is often a process on the path to progress, but it is also an effective way to refine and select the best.

Imagine you are a parent with X-ray vision, able to see inside your child’s brain. Every morning, you would find that the synapses in your child’s brain have decreased a little more. You would panic and say, “This is wrong, the synapses are decreasing more and more. Something must be wrong; we must do something, we need to see a doctor!” But fortunately, you don’t have X-ray vision. What you see is merely the process of the child’s normal growth.

Economics, markets, and personal careers usually follow a similar path—a process of continuous growth amidst constant losses.

You need short-term vigilance to survive so that you can see the long-term optimistic results.

Chapter 7: Freedom#

Time freedom is the greatest dividend that wealth can bring you.

The highest form of wealth is when you wake up every morning and can say, “Today, I can do anything I want to do.”

If happiness had a common denominator—a universal source of joy—it would be complete control over life.

The freedom to do what you want, when you want, and for as long as you want, is extremely precious, and this is the greatest dividend that money can bring us.

John D. Rockefeller was one of the most successful businessmen in history. He was also a recluse, spending most of his time alone. He was taciturn, minimizing social interaction, and even if you caught his attention, he would remain silent.

A refinery worker who became one of Rockefeller’s confidants once said, “He let everyone speak, but he himself leaned back in his chair, silent.”

When someone asked him why he remained silent in meetings, Rockefeller often quoted a poem to answer:

A wise old owl lives in an oak tree,

The more it sees, the less it speaks,

The less it speaks, the more it hears,

Why can’t we be like this wise old owl?

Chapter 8: The Luxury Car Paradox#

There is a paradox here: we all want to use wealth to tell others that we deserve their admiration and respect. But in reality, others often skip the step of admiring you. This is not because they feel your wealth is unworthy of envy, but because they use your wealth as a yardstick to express their own desire to be admired and respected.

You might think you need an expensive car, a luxury watch, and a big house, but I want to tell you that you don’t truly want these things themselves. What you truly want is the respect and envy from others. You think that owning expensive things will make others respect and envy you, but unfortunately, they won’t—especially the people whose respect and envy you desire.

Chapter 9: The Wealth You Cannot See#

A person’s wealth is invisible because wealth is what remains from income that has not been spent. Wealth exists before you make a purchasing decision. Its value lies in the choices, flexibility, and room for growth it provides you, allowing you to buy more things in the future than you can buy now.

Chapter 15: There Is No Free Lunch in Life#

“Any job looks simple to an observer.”

Volatility is akin to a transaction fee, not a penalty. Market returns are never free, and they can never be free. They demand a price from you, just like any other product. You are not forced to pay, just as you are not forced to go to Disneyland. You could go to a local county fair with a $10 ticket, or stay home for free. Some Thoughts#

How to Achieve Financial Freedom by Trading Stocks#

Buffett’s True Secret to Success#

  • Buffett does not borrow heavily.
  • Buffett has survived 14 economic downturns, but none of them made him panic-sell stocks.
  • Buffett never tarnishes his business reputation.
  • Buffett never clings to one strategy, one worldview, or one trend; he is always learning and never goes all-in on one thing.
  • Buffett does not govern the company using other people’s money.
  • Buffett has not exhausted himself, nor has he quit or retired.

Buffett repeatedly emphasizes the so-called “margin of safety.” His strategy requires that even during a massive bull market, you must always hold some cash. When everyone else is fully invested and earning a 9% return, your cash might only yield 1% return. This feeling is difficult, but you must endure this discomfort to ensure that during a bear market, you still have money and are not forced to sell stocks.

Making Money is One Thing, Preserving Wealth is Another.#

It is foolish to take risks with the money you have and need in order to make money you don’t have and don’t need. To make money they didn’t have, and didn’t need the risk what they did have and did need and that’s foolish. It is reasonable to invest a small portion of income without affecting the quality of life. But people must be careful of the “Never Enough” impulse and should not take excessive risks (leveraging investments, shorting stocks, stepping over legal lines, etc.) to pursue more wealth; instead, they should focus on preserving wealth and slowly increasing it through the power of compound interest for sustainable development.

Taking Risks Can Make You Money, But It Can Also Make You Lose It.#

  • Making money relies on luck, optimism, and willingness to take risks.
  • Preserving money requires avoiding risks, extreme humility, and having fear in your heart.

The Straight-A Student vs. The Treasure Hunter#

The Straight-A Student#

This mindset is like those good students in school who hope to get good grades in every subject and fear having any weak points. If this person does ten things, they expect to do at least nine well, and they will apologize for the one that wasn’t done well.

But the straight-A student mindset is actually an employee mindset. Everything you do is pre-arranged and must be done. So, if you don’t know what to do right now, or what the consequences of each action will be. If you are an entrepreneur, an investor, or a leader, this employee mindset won’t work. You have to select a few things from a large pile of potentially beneficial things.

The Treasure Hunter#

Those who invest know that investment portfolios have a strong tail effect. Often, most of the projects you invest in are not successful, but a few will be extremely successful, and almost all of your returns come from those extremely successful projects. This effect is the “80/20 Rule” in investing. Moreover, this “investment” is broad, not limited to stock trading.

The key to the treasure hunter mindset is casting a wide net. If you estimate there might be gems in this mine, can you say you only explored the spot with gems? Can you say you only bought the pit with gems? You must control the entire large area. Football schools, celebrity agencies, copyright representation, IP adaptation—all follow this mindset.

We live in a world where “good things” are distributed very unevenly.

  • In your lifetime, perhaps only a few books will have the deepest impact on you.
  • Perhaps only one or two skills will determine your future.
  • Perhaps only a few events will change your destiny.
  • Perhaps there are only a few people who are most important to you.

You must cast a wide net, try many things, and expand your search range, not fearing the waste of time and money, to ensure you find the best few.

If you only want to do the right things, the things proven by history, your path will become narrower and narrower.

Why Ambitious People Live in Disappointment of Themselves#

These are truly “gray” days. You didn’t sell at the last peak, and you don’t know when the next peak will come; the current point only brings you disappointment. These are the days when a top student scores 97 points.

Investing is an activity that everyone can participate in, and it provides direct feedback on your decisions. In particular, investing shows whether you can face the real world.

So, if you are an ambitious person, your days are spent pursuing occasional satisfaction amidst disappointment.
