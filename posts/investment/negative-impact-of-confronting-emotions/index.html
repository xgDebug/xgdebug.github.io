The negative impact of confronting emotions

This paper explores seven mindsets that lead to erroneous investment decisions, including greed, fear, self-deception, herd mentality, jealousy, overconfidence, and the capitulation mindset. It reveals how these psychological factors universally affect investors and trigger frequent and repetitive behavioral errors.

The desire to earn more, the fear of missing out on opportunities, the personality trait of comparing oneself to others, the influence of the group, and the dream of successful investment—these factors are almost universally present. Consequently, they exert a profound collective influence on most investors and the market, resulting in errors. These errors are frequent, widespread, and tend to repeat themselves.

Seven Mindsets Leading to Errors in Decision-Making#

Many people possess the intelligence to analyze data, but few are able to observe things deeply and withstand the powerful influence of psychological factors. In other words, many people arrive at similar conclusions based on their own analysis and cognition, but they act in drastically different ways due to varying psychological influences. The biggest investment bias does not come from information or analysis, but from mindset.

Greed#

Greed is defined as “an excessive or exhaustive desire, and usually a reprehensible possessiveness, especially for wealth or profit.”

Greed is an extremely powerful force. Its power is so great that it can override common sense, risk aversion, caution, logic, the memory of past painful lessons, determination, panic, and other elements that would normally make an investor avoid trouble. Furthermore, greed occasionally leads investors to join the pursuit of profit, only to pay the price in the end.

Fear#

Fear, like greed, implies excess. Therefore, fear is more akin to panic. Fear is an excessive worry that causes investors to remain inactive when they should be taking positive action.

Self-Deception#

Nothing is easier to self-deceive than oneself, because everyone, as long as they wish to, will believe it is true. Investors are always looking for a panacea, calling it the “Holy Grail” or the “Free Lunch,” but everyone wants a ticket that turns them into a wealthy person without taking any risks. Few question whether this ticket exists, or why they should receive it. Most importantly, the average person always maintains hope.

What factors make people believe in a panacea?

  1. First, there is usually a factual basis for it.
  2. Then, a set of seemingly intelligent theories is fabricated, which the believers promote everywhere to persuade others. Subsequently, this theory accidentally generates profits, either because the theory has some value or simply because new believers join and drive up the price of the target asset. Finally, it appears to be a definite way to acquire wealth, and this creates a craze.

Herd Mentality (Conformity)#

Not even when one insists on their own opinion, even if the crowd’s view is clearly absurd. Time and time again, the pressure of the herd and the desire to get rich cause people to abandon independent judgment and skepticism, suppressing their innate risk-averse mindset to believe in the unreasonable. Since this situation occurs so regularly, something must be at work, rather than merely random influence.

Envy#

Even if the force of greed is negative, it at least motivates others to strive for more profit. But the negative influence of comparing oneself to others is even stronger. This is what we consider the most dangerous trait of human nature.

A person who might be happy when isolated can feel miserable when they see others doing better. In the investment world, you will find that most investors find it difficult to sit idly by and watch others earn more money than them.

Hubris (Overconfidence)#

It is an enormous challenge to remain objective and prudent when facing the following realities:

  1. We evaluate and compare short-term investment performance.
  2. During a bull market, making an inappropriate, even reckless, decision to increase risk often yields the best returns (and most of the time, it is a bull market).
  3. The best returns bring the greatest reward. When things go right, feeling smart and having others agree with it is a very pleasant feeling.

In contrast, a thoughtful investor works diligently, earning stable returns during a bull market and losing less than others during a bear market. They avoid high-risk behavior because they know there is much they do not understand, and they suppress their hubris. In my view, this is the best formula for long-term wealth creation, but it does not satisfy the mindset in the short term. Emphasizing humility, caution, and risk control is not a glamorous path, and of course, investment should not seek glamour, but that is what people often think.

Surrender Mentality#

This is an investment behavior that is consistently observed in the later stages of a cycle. Investors try their best to stick to their beliefs, but when they cannot resist economic or psychological pressure, they surrender and follow the trend. Often, asset prices are too high and continue to rise, or the price is too low and continues to fall. Eventually, these trends shake the investor’s mindset, beliefs, and determination. Others profit because you refuse to buy a stock, while the stock you choose to buy hovers at a low price every day. Furthermore, the investment concept you originally thought was unsafe and unwise seems to be going in the right direction—selling the overpriced stock and buying the underpriced stock—but the investor does not do it. The tendency toward self-doubt, mixed with news of others’ success, forms a powerful force that leads the investor to make the wrong decision. And when this trend continues for a longer period, the force becomes stronger—this is another influence factor we must fight against.
