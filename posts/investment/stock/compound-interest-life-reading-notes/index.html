《Compound Interest Life》 Reading Notes

Exploring key questions in investment: Is it a one-time capital investment, or phased/gradual investment? Through historical data, market behavior, and psychological analysis, we reveal the core principles of long-term investment.
April 28, 2025 · 3 min · xgDebug | Translations:
  • 中文

Should You Invest Lump Sum or Invest Periodically? When you receive an inheritance or another large sum of money, have you ever wondered whether to invest it all at once, or spread the money out and invest it gradually? History over the past 150 years tells us that the North American stock market rises in about two out of every three years. From a statistical perspective, the longer your money stays in the market, the more likely it is to grow. Therefore, the solution to this dilemma is to invest lump sum, while maintaining the awareness that the market could fall at any time. If you are uneasy about investing lump sum, you can set up a simple system for incremental investing (for example, investing 25% of the total amount on the first day of every month, continuing this for four months).

While trillions of dollars are managed by Wall Street professionals who charge high management fees, it is usually the managers who reap massive profits, not the clients.

Don’t think you know more than the market; no one knows more than the market. Don’t act based on your own opinion; you think it’s just your idea, but in reality, millions of people think the same way.

The reason the power of compounding is so counterintuitive is that we rarely view time as an ally. Over time, everything around us seems to degrade and lose value. The top-tier computer bought a few years ago starts slowing down; the house requires expensive maintenance to withstand the elements; even our bodies age. In investing, the situation is exactly the opposite. Investing is one of the few areas where time is on our side.

Investing is frustrating because we always feel we could be doing better. If we invest during a market downturn, we will be disappointed when the market rises sharply because we didn’t invest more. If the market falls after we invest, we will regret it due to bad luck, saying we should have waited longer.

This emotion is widespread, and as investors, we need to recognize it. We can always do better, even if we are right, even if we achieve good returns—we can always do better. Investing is almost certainly going to be disappointing, at least in the short term.

If paying attention to the latest economic developments could make people rich, journalists would already be millionaires. Dear reader, let me tell you a secret: journalists are not millionaires!

News applications aim to draw you into their echo chamber from your phone’s home screen, allowing them to display ads and monitor your behavior. For you, the news is not important information; it is bait that pulls you out of your life and traps you. Turn it off.

In my opinion, the most insidious articles are those that explain why a certain company’s stock rose or fell. These articles are titled things like, “Bank of America Stock Falls Today, Here’s Why,” or “Three Reasons Why Netflix Stock Plunged.” The tone of these articles often makes us think that the author knew the decline was coming and graciously typed out an explanation for us—a small miracle. This makes people even more convinced that predicting a decline is possible. In fact, the authors of these articles don’t know the stock movement; they are merely being “after-the-fact Zhuge Liang” (a reference to a brilliant strategist who often appears after the event), doing so to attract investor clicks.

What smart investors do is forget what might happen and prepare for possibilities through diversification, rational asset allocation, and patience.

In the short term, the stock market is like cocaine—never let it affect you. In most cases, company profits grow over time, and that is the key. What is important is the systematic nature of the investment. Therefore, becoming a good investor requires extreme self-discipline.

In my line of work, the money earned from selling advice is more than the money earned from following it. This is one of the conditions for the magazine industry to survive, the other being the reader’s short memory.

Warren Buffett once said that even if epoch-making disasters like war or pandemics occur, we should not stop investing. In a letter to shareholders, he recounted how his 11-year-old self bought his first batch of stocks on March 11, 1942, three months after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor.

In the three days before Buffett bought his first stock, the headline of The New York Times was “Japanese Forces Break Through Malayan Defenses.” The next day, the paper announced: “Japanese Forces Invade New Guinea from Two Points, Occupy Yangon, and Advance on the Western Front of Burma.” The news on the third day was: “Enemy Artillery Approaches Australia, 98,000 Surrendered in Java, Reportedly.”

Times are always uncertain; violent events constantly threaten world peace, and the risk of economic recession and depression always accompanies us. Here is a brief summary of negative events over the past decade:

  • Russia and Ukraine launched a large-scale conflict, resulting in thousands of deaths.
  • Severe riots occurred at the Capitol Building in Washington D.C.
  • The COVID-19 pandemic caused millions of deaths, leading to a stock market crash and global economic recession.
  • Rebel armed groups attacked oil refineries in Saudi Arabia.

Long-term optimists predict that the world collapses about once every decade.

A stock market crash is like lightning; it can leave even the most rational people stunned. However, just as lightning ushered in the electric age, a stock market crash should be welcomed by most of us.

Many people think they can handle a stock market crash without panicking. But a stock market crash is not just a notification popping up on a phone screen. We calculate our losses in our minds, which might be equivalent to several months or even years of our salary. Only then do we feel genuine panic.

The S&P 500 index averages three drops of 5% or more per year.

Over the past 100 years, the S&P 500 index has experienced a 10% drop approximately every 16 months. Over the last century, a 20% drop occurs on average every seven years. Since the 1950s, the S&P 500 index has also experienced three drops of about 50%, averaging once every 22 years. This famous “market volatility” is so common that we shouldn’t be surprised by it, yet it always surprises us!

The losses caused by downturns are usually temporary. For example, since World War II, the market has averaged only four months to recover from a maximum 20% drop and return to its previous level.

Market returns are never free; they never will be in the future either.

A market adjustment is not a flaw in the financial system. Accepting that investing involves losses is the price you pay for long-term growth. No adjustment, no risk; no risk, no reward.

Setting new highs is the norm, not the exception, in the stock market. Therefore, if you delay investing because the market is strong or at a new high, you might be delaying for a long time!

Since 1928, the S&P 500 index has set a new historical high, on average, every 20 trading days.

From 1926 to 2019, the S&P 500 index rose for nearly three out of every four years. What about the year after a bull market? The index still rises…

After astonishing and absurd surges, such as when the index rises by 50% in 12 months, it should obviously pull back, right? But that’s not the case. Historically, after an incredible year of gains, the market’s return in the second year is indeed negative, with an average return of -1.5%. But after that year of 50% gains, the average market return three years later is 42%, and five years later, it is 66%, not including dividend payments.

If investing were easy, everyone would be rich. Investing shouldn’t be easy; those who think it is easy are fools.
