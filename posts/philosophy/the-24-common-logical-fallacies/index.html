The 24 Common Logical Fallacies

June 18, 2023 · 7 min · xgDebug | Translations:
  • 中文

Distorting Viewpoint

Literal Translation: Straw Man Other Names: Straw Dummy / Misrepresentation

It is the act of distorting an opponent’s arguments to make them easier to attack.

By exaggerating, twisting, or even completely fabricating an opponent’s arguments, one presents their own position as more reasonable. However, sincere and rational debate is destroyed by this form of argumentation, which is essentially fraud.

Example: Xiao Ming says: “The state should invest more funds in the medical and education sectors.” Xiao Liang replies: “That’s terrifying! You actually hate the country so much, that you want to reduce defense spending, leaving our army defenseless and unable to protect the nation.”

Note: The original English term for this fallacy, “strawman,” literally translates to “straw dummy.” Since a straw dummy is not a real person, it won’t offer any resistance in a fight, and it is very easy to knock down. In the UK, this fallacy is also often called “Aunt Sally.”

Causal Attribution Error

It is concluding that a causal relationship exists between two things, either genuinely existing or hypothetically assumed, based on the association between them.

It is assuming that a causal relationship exists between two things simply because they are associated, or merely because they exist simultaneously. Different things may occur simultaneously or sequentially, but this does not necessarily mean that one is the cause of the other. Sometimes, the association between two things is purely coincidental, and it is even possible that both things share the same cause.

Example: Xiao Ming points to a table and says, “Over the past few centuries, global temperatures have been rising, and at the same time, the number of pirates has been decreasing. Therefore, we can conclude: the existence of pirates lowers global temperatures, and global warming is a hoax.”

Emotional Blackmail (or Emotional Manipulation)

It is substituting genuine, effective arguments with the manipulation of emotions.

Emotional blackmail includes manipulating fear, jealousy, hatred, pity, pride, and so on. There is one very important thing we must know: sometimes a logically sound argument may trigger a strong emotional response. But abandoning logic and relying solely on appeals to emotion to mask the fact that one’s own viewpoint lacks strong evidence will only lead to more problems and fallacies. Every mentally healthy person is influenced by emotions, so emotional blackmail is a very common and effective debate strategy, but fundamentally, it is a flawed and hypocritical way of arguing, and it tends to rationalize the emotional state of the other party in the debate.

Example: Xiao Liang dislikes drinking mutton soup, but his father tells him, “Think about the children in poor African countries who are starving to death, those pitiful kids who can’t eat anything.”

Fallacy Leading to Error (or Fallacious Conclusion)

Literal Translation: Fallacy Fallacy Other Names: Mistaking Error for Error / Assuming Error

It is judging that a conclusion must be wrong simply because the level of argumentation is poor, or because the argument contains errors.

It is not logically sound to assume that a viewpoint must lead to a fallacious conclusion; even incorrect premises can be presented using sound logic. Similarly, it is entirely possible to prove a correct viewpoint using flawed premises in a debate, or to include logical fallacies in the process of argumentation.

Example: Xiao Li discovers that Xiao Hong advocates for a healthy diet merely because some dubious nutritionist claims, “A healthy diet is a popular lifestyle,” which is unreliable. But Xiao Li then concludes, “Therefore, the conclusion that a healthy diet is necessary is wrong, and binge eating every day is fine,” which is also a fallacy.

Slippery Slope

If A happens, then eventually bad thing Z will happen, so we cannot allow A to happen.

The problem is that this form of reasoning avoids discussing the current issue A, instead shifting the focus to the hypothetical extreme assumption Z. Furthermore, there is no evidence to prove that Z will necessarily happen because of A. This fallacy is also a form of emotional blackmail, using fear to undermine the fairness of the current discussion with unverified speculation.

Example: Xiao Qiang asserts that if we allow same-sex marriage, the next thing that will happen is that we will allow incestuous marriage, and even allow humans to marry monkeys or humans to marry cars.

Ad Hominem (Attacking the Person)

It is diminishing an opponent’s argument by attacking their personal character or other personal traits, such as height, appearance, social status, etc.

Ad hominem can be a public, blatant attack, or it can be a subtle implication that the opponent’s character is questionable, thereby discrediting their argument. In an argument, ad hominem often achieves the effect of defeating the opponent without a fight.

Example: Huihui presents undeniable evidence showing that the new tax system is fairer, more reasonable, and worth promoting. Zhuang asks the attendees: “Do you think you should believe anything said by a woman who is unmarried, has a criminal record, and smells like body odor?”

Tu Quoque (You Too)

Responding to criticism by criticizing the critic: To avoid directly addressing the criticism, one turns the accusation back onto the person who raised the criticism.

The original Latin term for this fallacy is tu quoque, meaning “you too (have done it).” This logical fallacy is commonly known as “hypocrisy of the critic.” It is usually a diversionary tactic that allows the party who must defend their argument to shift the focus from themselves back onto the person who raised the criticism.

Example: At the hospital, Xiao Mei points out that Xiao Li’s poor health condition is due to her failure to strictly follow the doctor’s orders. Instead of reflecting on why she didn’t follow the orders, Xiao Li blames Xiao Mei: “Don’t talk about me! You never listened to the doctor’s advice either; you never took your medicine on time!”

Note: In China, this fallacy is also known as the “Flea Theory” (臭虫论): Lu Xun, in his 1933 article “Foreigners Also Have,” satirized how Chinese people, when criticized, would claim that other countries also have the same errors to cover up their own mistakes. “All of China has it; foreign countries have it too. Foreigners say China has fleas, but the West also has fleas.”

Personal Limitation

Literal Translation: Self-Doubt

Due to one’s own level of understanding, one is unable or finds it difficult to comprehend something, and thus concludes that the matter might be wrong or false.

For discussions on certain complex topics, such as “biological evolution and natural selection,” one needs a basic knowledge base on the subject. Simply because one does not know this knowledge, one assumes that these theories must be wrong—this is a fallacy.

Example: Xiao Ming draws a fish and a human, and dismissively asks Xiao Qiang: “Look at these two creatures. Do you really think I would be stupid enough to believe that a fish can randomly transform into a human over time?”

Selective Exception (or Cherry Picking)

When a certain viewpoint is proven to be wrong, one changes the rules of discussion or uses a special exception to excuse the error.

Foolish humans are always reluctant to admit they are wrong. They are not only unwilling to improve their understanding of the problem through better comprehension, but sometimes they stubbornly try to find ways to prove their own correctness. The common method is to find a rationalizing reason for their error after the fact. It is easy to find a reason to believe in what we are accustomed to, but the real difficulty lies in how we test our own worldview after others have confirmed our error, and how we correct our thoughts.

Example: Xiao Ming claims he can predict the future. When everyone tests his predictions in a reasonable scientific environment, he retreats. Xiao Ming says you must first believe that he has precognitive ability before you can see his predictions come true.

Presupposition Questioning (or Leading Question)

Also called “Inductive Questioning,” this involves pre-inserting an assumption into a question, making it so that the respondent, no matter how they answer, appears to be in the wrong.

Presupposition questioning is particularly effective in undermining rational debate because it is provocative. The respondent is often forced to panic and defend themselves.

For example: Xiao Mei and Xiao Li both like Ah Hui. One time, while Ah Hui was present, Xiao Mei deliberately asked Xiao Li in a curious tone: “Xiao Li, have you stopped gambling now?” (The question presupposes that Xiao Li used to gamble.)

Burden of Proof

Assuming that the responsibility of providing evidence lies not with the person proposing the view, but with the person questioning the view.

The person who makes the claim must provide the evidence; the responsibility should not be thrown onto the person who doubts the argument. Just because a view cannot be falsified or no counterexample can be presented does not automatically prove that the view is reasonable or credible. At the same time, we must clearly understand that “nothing is absolute,” so we need to strengthen the credibility of the argument based on known evidence. Dismissing a view simply because it hasn’t been proven is also a fallacy of inference.

Example: Xiao Qiang says he can see ghosts. Just because other people cannot see ghosts does not prove whether his statement is true or not.

Double Entendre

Using a statement with a double meaning or an ambiguous statement that contains ambiguity to mislead or distort the facts.

Politicians often utilize ambiguous statements, and when they are exposed, they point out that, theoretically, what they said was not entirely a lie. But in essence, these statements are misleading from the very beginning.

Example: Zhuang asks A Cong: “You always say you’ll treat me to a meal. When can you fulfill that promise?” A Cong replies: “I’ll go buy you a packet of instant noodles in a bit.”

Gambler’s Fallacy

Believing that there are predictable patterns in independent, random events.

It is like the winning rate of a roulette wheel. Generally speaking, the probability of a single repeated result occurring is extremely low, and the location of the ball each time is completely unrelated to the previous time. For instance, although there is a tiny possibility of twenty consecutive coin flips all landing on heads, the probability of each individual flip landing on heads is still 50/50, and this probability is not affected by the previous flips.

Example: When rolling dice, six consecutive “Big” numbers appear. Zhuang believes that the next roll must definitely be “Small.” As a result, he loses everything.

Blind Conformity (or Bandwagon Effect)

Literal Translation: Bandwagon

Believing that something is correct simply because many people are doing it or agreeing with it.

The fallacy of this viewpoint lies in the fact that the popularity or acceptance of something is completely unrelated to whether it is right or wrong. If we decide right or wrong based on public acceptance, then in the era when people believed the Earth was flat, the Earth should have been flat.

Example: Drunken Old Wang points to Old Zhang and says: “If monsters are just foolish superstitions, why do so many people still believe they truly exist?”

Note: The original English term for this fallacy, “bandwagon,” generally refers to the magnificent carriage carrying a band in a circus parade, and the derived meaning is “a trend or fashion accepted by the public.”

Appeal to Authority

The belief that what an authority figure says must be correct.

It is important to remember that this fallacy does not mean that the claims of a reasonable expert or scientific consensus are unimportant; rather, it means that appealing to authority is not a valid form of argumentation, and the authority’s opinion can also be completely wrong. Therefore, whether someone is an authority or not has no essential impact on whether their claim is true. However, unless a person has a level of understanding or empirical evidence similar to that of an expert, it is unreasonable to disregard the statement of someone with deep knowledge.

Example: Old Zhang doesn’t know how to defend his position that evolution “is not real,” so he says: “I know a famous scientist, Zhuang Zhuang, who also questions evolution!” In fact, Zhuang Zhuang is just a successful fitness coach.

Hasty Generalization

Literal Translation: Combination Division (or Part-to-Whole)

Assuming that a local characteristic of something is the overall characteristic of that thing, or that it applies to other parts of that thing; or that the overall characteristic of an object must apply to each of its independent parts.

This fallacy is also called the “Fallacy of Composition/Division.” Usually, when something is correct for a part, it is indeed possible that it applies to the whole, and vice versa—like saying, “Peeking through a tube, one can see a glimpse.” But the key point is whether there is effective evidence to show that the situation is indeed like that. Simply observing certain consistencies in something may lead to biased thinking, causing us to assume that the consistency exists where it actually does not.

Example: Xiao Cong is a precocious child with logical thinking. He knows that atoms, which are invisible to the naked eye, make up him, so he concludes that he cannot be seen. Unfortunately, despite his thoughts, he still lost the game of hide-and-seek.

Purifying the Argument (or Moving the Goalposts)

Literal Translation: Not a True Scotsman

It is the act of adding premises after the fact, constantly modifying or “purifying” the argument, to refute criticism of your argument’s flaws.

In this form of faulty reasoning, no matter how convincing the evidence is for someone’s belief, simply by changing the standard, the evidence can be made inapplicable to the fabricated so-called “true” example. This post-hoc rationalization is a way of avoiding reasonable criticism of one’s own argument.

Example: A Liang says that Cantonese people only eat sweet tofu pudding. A Hui says that he is Cantonese, but he eats savory tofu pudding. A Liang gets particularly angry and tells A Hui: “True Cantonese people only eat sweet tofu pudding!”

Note: The original English term for this fallacy is “no true Scotsman,” meaning “not a true Scotsman.” It comes from the work On Thinking by Professor Anthony Flew, published in 1975, where he cited a Scotsman who, in order to deny that Scotsmen commit serious crimes, said, “No true Scotsman would do such a thing.”

Genetic Determinism (or Origin Determinism)

Literal Translation: Gene Determines

Judging the good or bad, right or wrong of something based on its origin or the person who proposed the viewpoint.

Also called genetic determinism: Good people and bad people are predetermined in their genes, such as, “You are inherently a bad person, so you are destined only to do bad things and never do good things.” It attempts to evade positive debate by focusing on the origin of a person or thing. Similar to the “Ad Hominem” fallacy, it attacks and discredits the opponent’s viewpoint by utilizing known negative perceptions, yet it doesn’t actually explain what is wrong with the opponent’s argument.

Example: A news channel plays a seismic alert message. Xiao Liang comforts Xiao Hong, saying, “We all know that the news channel, besides celebrity gossip, never broadcasts serious news.”

False Dichotomy (or Black or White)

Only offering two options as choices, while completely ignoring the existence of other possibilities.

Also called “either/or,” “dilemma reasoning,” or “false reasoning.” This insidious strategy superficially presents a logical argument, but upon closer inspection, one can find that there are many more possibilities than the binary choices presented. The binary, black-or-white mindset does not allow for the existence of various variables, conditions, and contexts beyond two possibilities. It only misleads the argument and blocks rational, honest debate.

Example: The Supreme Commander says: “Anyone who doesn’t support his grand blueprint of ‘Making America Great Again’ is an enemy of America.”

Circular Reasoning

The premise of the argument already contains the conclusion.

Such self-contained logic is usually rooted in deep-seated, obsessive assumptions. For example, the cliché: “The main reason circular reasoning is bad is because it is bad.”

Example: The Bullshit Journal is flawless. We know this because The Bullshit Journal itself once stated: “Everything in The Bullshit Journal is true and beautiful; you must not have any doubts.”

Appeal to Nature

Simply because something is “natural” or “of nature,” one assumes it is perfect, and its rationality and effectiveness are indisputable.

Many naturally occurring things are considered beneficial, which may cause our cognition to become biased. Nature itself does not make things good or bad. For example, killing in nature can be seen as very “natural,” but this does not mean that killing is beneficial or rational.

Example: Zhuang believes in “natural remedies,” so when he has a cold or fever, he only drinks plain boiled water, and when he is sick, he only eats herbs. Because he believes that plain boiled water or herbs are more natural than chemically synthesized drugs, they must be more beneficial.

Anecdotal Evidence (or Hearsay)

Using personal experience or isolated cases to replace irrefutable evidence or sound arguments.

People are usually more inclined to believe the statements of others than to understand complex data and changes as a whole. Quantitative scientific measurement is almost always more accurate than personal perception and experience, but we tend to believe things that are tangibly present to us, and what people we trust say, rather than the reality shown by what looks more “abstract” statistics.

Example: Xiao Jie’s grandfather smokes 30 cigarettes a day and still lives to 97 years old. Therefore, Xiao Jie concludes that the research findings proving a causal relationship between smoking and lung cancer cannot be trusted.

Causal Displacement (or Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc)

Literal Translation: Texas Sharpshooter

Only selecting evidence that favors oneself or matches one’s own hypothesis.

Although shooting first and then drawing a target center on the bullet hole makes the shooter look like a sharpshooter, there is no necessary causal relationship between the bullet hitting the bullseye and the shooter being a sharpshooter.

Example: Coca-Cola claims that research shows that in the five countries where Coca-Cola sells the most, three countries are among the top ten globally for average IQ. Therefore, drinking Coca-Cola can improve IQ.

Note: The original English term for this fallacy is “the Texas sharpshooter,” referring to a Texas shooter who shoots first and then draws a target.

Middle Ground (or Compromise)

The compromise, or middle ground, between two extremes must certainly be the location of the truth.

Often, the truth does lie between two extreme points, but this can also cause us to develop a cognitive bias: sometimes something is fundamentally wrong, even if it is a compromise. The middle ground between truth and fallacy is still a fallacy.

Example: Xiao Li firmly believes that vaccination causes autism in children, but A Cong, her friend who likes research, says that this claim has been scientifically proven to be false. Then their friend Xiao Mei proposes a compromise argument: Vaccination inevitably causes certain types of autism, not all types of autism.
