Understanding People, Events, and Situations: 30 Survival Wisdoms#

See through the interests behind rhetoric, identify the costs hidden beneath grand narratives, and maintain the capacity for independent thought.

One of the greatest rewards of reading history is realizing that while eras, figures, and excuses change, the underlying logic remains surprisingly consistent.

People package self-interest as sentimentality, conceal costs under ideals, guilt-trip others using morality, and justify immediate sacrifices with grand narratives.

Below are 30 survival experiences worth remembering in the long run. They are not absolute truths, but signals to remain vigilant when observing the world and identifying risks.

I. On Organizations and Power#

1. If a product starts emphasizing patriotism and sentimentality, be alert: it likely no longer intends to focus seriously on quality and design.

Sentimentality can add value, but it can never replace product quality. Truly exceptional products speak through quality and design first.

2. If an organization heavily stresses past glory while painting a vision of a wonderful future, be alert: it may be encouraging you to sacrifice the present.

Pay special attention to who gets to enjoy that bright future, and who bears the immediate costs today.

3. If an organization repeatedly emphasizes “the big picture” without ever clarifying whose interests actually constitute it, it most likely expects you to sacrifice yours.

Ask clearly: Who built this frame, and who is paying the bill for it?

4. If someone constantly demands that you show vision and selflessness, yet rarely mentions their own responsibilities and contributions, their so-called vision usually means letting you foot their bill.

Do not easily accept moral standards that bind only others while leaving their proponents unrestrained.

5. If an organization heavily stresses loyalty without emphasizing its responsibilities to its members, such loyalty is often a one-way street.

A truly healthy relationship must be reciprocal: you have duties to the organization, and the organization has duties to you.

6. If a system constantly stresses stability while forbidding the discussion of problems and correction of mistakes, it may be defending vested interests rather than order.

Genuine stability comes from solving problems, not making them disappear from discussion.

7. If leadership emphasizes process when things go wrong but highlights personal contribution when success is achieved, you should be wary of this accountability mechanism.

When evaluating an organization, look not only at how it assigns credit, but more importantly at how it allocates responsibility.

8. If an organization only allows you to share in success but forbids you from questioning decisions, you are not a participant; you are a managed resource.

Participation means having the right to express opinions, raise questions, and influence decisions.

II. On Business and Money#

9. If a merchant constantly emphasizes costs, sentiment, and hardships without disclosing the price breakdown, they may want you to replace business judgment with sympathy.

Consumers have no moral obligation to bear a business’s operational difficulties.

10. If an investment project constantly emphasizes FOMO (“you’ll regret missing out today”) rather than explaining risks and cash flow, it is likely selling you anxiety rather than an opportunity.

Projects truly worth investing in withstand calm analysis and do not rely on manufacturing urgency to close a deal.

11. If a business model relies on constantly recruiting new members for early members to profit, you should first ask whether you are a consumer or the next target for recruitment.

Focus on investigating whether profits derive from real products and services, or from the capital brought in by newcomers.

12. If a product constantly emphasizes “limited edition,” “last chance,” or “exclusive spots,” don’t rush to buy—think about why it needs to rob you of time to think.

Manufacturing scarcity is one of the most common tactics in sales psychology.

13. If a company constantly claims it is burning cash, educating the market, and changing the world, yet can never explain how it will achieve sustainable profitability, its vision may just be a curtain hiding flaws in its business model.

A grand market landscape does not equal a sound business model, and growth does not equal profitability.

III. On Interpersonal Relationships and Socializing#

14. If someone constantly uses “our years of friendship” to demand unreasonable favors, they may not miss the friendship itself, but rather the convenience it brings them.

True friendship is not a license for unlimited demands.

15. If someone constantly says “I’m doing this for your own good” while forbidding you from making your own choices, they may care less about your well-being and more about control.

Genuine care respects your autonomy rather than deciding your life for you.

16. If someone constantly highlights how much hardship they’ve endured and how wronged they’ve been, beware whether they are setting the stage to demand compensation from you later.

Other people’s hardships deserve respect, but that doesn’t mean you are obliged to pay for them.

17. If someone shows up only when they need you and disappears when you need help, do not mistake this relationship for friendship.

To judge a relationship, look not just at how passionate they are when needing you, but where they are when you need help.

18. If others always urge you to endure and yield, but never ask the takers to step back, their so-called tolerance is merely enabling unfairness.

True tolerance must be mutual, not a cost borne solely by the vulnerable side.

IV. On Education, Successology, and Social Concepts#

19. If an education system emphasizes only obedience, discipline, and standard answers while rarely encouraging independent thought, it may be cultivating manageable individuals rather than effective problem solvers.

The value of education is not merely acquiring knowledge, but gaining the ability to make independent judgments and solve problems.

20. If someone attributes every failure entirely to your lack of effort, they may be evading how environment, resources, and rules shape outcomes.

Effort matters, but it is not a cure-all explanation for everything.

21. If a theory of success constantly uses rare cases of counter-attacks as examples while ignoring the vast number of failures, it may be using survivor bias to manufacture hope.

Look beyond the stories of winners—study those who worked just as hard but did not succeed.

22. If a culture treats enduring hardship itself as a virtue without questioning its value or necessity, it may be romanticizing inefficiency and unreasonable systems.

Suffering is not the goal. Finding reasonable methods to reduce unnecessary hardship is true wisdom.

23. If someone tells you “young people shouldn’t care too much about immediate gains,” you had better check first whether they have already secured their own immediate benefits.

Those advising others to take a long-term view are sometimes the very ones who care most about short-term gains.

V. On Information, Public Opinion, and Thinking Traps#

24. If a viewpoint relies on anger, humiliation, and tribalism to sustain itself, it likely cannot withstand calm discussion.

A truly persuasive argument should be able to stand up to facts and logic.

25. If someone attacks your motives, identity, and stance instead of refuting your evidence, they are likely trying to divert the focus of the discussion.

Do not let a debate shift from “Is what you’re saying correct?” to “Do you have the standing to say it?”

26. If a piece of news tells you only what to believe without revealing its source or evidence, do not rush to believe it.

Before accepting conclusions, inspect the source of information and the chain of evidence.

27. If a group constantly requires an external enemy to explain its failures, it may not want its members focusing on internal issues.

External threats sometimes exist, but they should not become a universal excuse for all internal problems.

28. If a narrative reduces complex issues to a duel between “good guys and bad guys,” it may be lowering your cognitive burden while stripping away your ability to judge independently.

The world is rarely just black and white. The more complex the issue, the more it requires case-by-case analysis.

VI. Summarizing These 30 Wisdoms into Five Principles of Judgment#

While specific rhetoric evolves, the logic underneath can generally be boiled down to five questions:

First, follow the interests. Who benefits from this narrative? Who bears the cost? Are the costs and benefits distributed fairly?

Second, check for symmetry of responsibility. Do they practice what they demand of others? Are rights balanced by obligations?

Third, look at actual delivery. Listen less to promises, slogans, and visions, and look more at delivered products, services, and results.

Fourth, consider the exit costs. If an organization, relationship, or transaction makes it difficult for you to leave easily, exercise extra caution.

Fifth, test if it can be questioned. Products, systems, and views backed by real confidence should not fear transparency, comparison, or criticism.

Conclusion: Always Retain the Ability to Think Independently#

History repeatedly demonstrates that what makes humans most vulnerable to exploitation is rarely a lack of knowledge, but rather emotions, fear, greed, honor, and the longing for a better future.

Sentimentality is fine, ideals are fine, and loyalty and dedication are fine too.

However, none of these noble things should ever serve as tools to evade responsibility, mask flaws, or shift costs onto others.

Remember one key takeaway in particular:

Whenever someone asks you to sacrifice the present for a better future, ask first: “When this future arrives, will I still be around? And what is the person making this request sacrificing?”

Stay kind, but remain vigilant.

Hold onto ideals, but do not abandon reality.

Respect history, but do not be hijacked by historical narratives.

Trust others, but retain the right to verify.

A mature individual does not become cynical after seeing through people, but rather remains capable of independent judgment, rational choice, and taking responsibility for their own life after recognizing the complexity of human nature.
